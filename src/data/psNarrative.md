## Why computer science (the choice)
- After high school I had two offers: chemistry near home in Beijing (long-time dream), or computing in Macao (three-hour flight; mainly known for employment prospects).
- Looking back the choice felt almost like tossing a coin; it landed on Macao.
- Leaving familiar life gave me free time, but freedom did not automatically tell me what to do with it.

## First-year exploration → rebuilt study habits
- Year one: tried many things beyond textbooks — Linux, cybersecurity, microcontrollers; some interests stayed only briefly.
- Several core modules (e.g. linear algebra) did not get enough attention: I could follow procedures without knowing what matrices would eventually be used for.
- Then I regrouped modules by memorisation / analytical argument / computational understanding, adjusted methods, and put scattered interests into temporary “storage.”
- Result: stayed within top ~10% of cohort; more important — broke and rebuilt habits, gained a solid subject foundation.

## Xiaomi internship — first Skill task (key story)
- First task: use Codex to turn an API into a Skill an Agent could call.
- At the time I did not truly understand what a Skill was; I mainly knew it involved Markdown docs, so I put my mentor’s request straight into the AI and thought the task was finished.
- Another model’s review showed the gap: only static documentation existed; scripts, resources, and supporting components were barely addressed.
- Lesson: the model had done exactly what a partially informed person asked — the real problem was my incomplete understanding.
- Recovery: restudied Skill structure, broke the task into smaller parts, asked Codex step by step; assigned models by strength (faster for generation, stricter for review).
- By the end of the internship I could contribute to enterprise AI Agent workflows, retrieval routing, and BI orchestration.
- That first setback stayed with me: when tools do more implementation, what depth of understanding do I need to decide what to ask and to judge whether the result is correct? Before engineering turns requests into code, what determines those requests?

## How I work with AI Agents (method)
- Examine the Agent’s execution trace as closely as the final output.
- Example: an Agent ran long while repeatedly processing similar content; after checking logs I stopped it and delivered a result that already covered main requirements — another loop / model call / more tokens was not buying a meaningfully better result.
- Project Management trade-off among time, scope, and cost became real when I pressed Stop.
- Later work: natural-language parsing, retrieval and Skill routing; compared BM25 vs vector retrieval for short queries.
- Method: first establish what I genuinely understand, then decide where the tool should begin helping.

## Beyond algorithms — product curiosity
- Actively spoke with colleagues in product, testing, and other roles.
- Developers / testers / algorithm engineers had concrete daily work; but when I asked who target users were, what problem mattered most, and where advantage lay, those answers were still taking shape.
- That prompted interest in seeing product decision-making more closely next, while still building technical depth — the first Skill task showed the cost of relying on tools too early without understanding the technology.
- Want to see what happens before a requirement reaches development/algorithm teams: discussions, judgements, trade-offs.

## Study abroad moments that shaped how I think
- University of Bologna Smart Objects and Applications Summer School: IoT and AI alongside energy and sustainability.
- Stuck on: if an AI system used to monitor energy consumes more resources than it helps save, what purpose does AI serve there?
- Extended unease to generative AI in writing, slides, planning, academic work — and to research: if AI drafts and another AI reviews, which parts of the process must remain mine?
- Shift: less excitement from “new/powerful” alone; more attention to what happens after a technology is put to use.
- Gardens by the Bay, Singapore: met a computing student from India who loved photography; despite walking difficulty she kept finding better angles for someone she had just met; later learned it was her first trip abroad.
- Remember her face talking about photography more than field talk — became more willing to listen to how other people live; less quick to take things for granted.

## Human-Centered AI — ocean and net (core view)
- Spark: Data Driven Decision Management. The teacher said human thinking will not be replaced by AI. After using AI in work and research, I became more sure of that.
- Pushback to panic: as models grow stronger in modeling, research, and writing, more people fear replacement. I do not think AI replaces people.
- Metaphor I use: today’s AI is like an ocean — deeper and wider than any one person. The ocean is vast but unstructured.
- A person’s role is to weave that ocean into a net.
- Net as connection: move ideas across domains that look unrelated; carry tech into places raw data alone cannot reach.
- Net as boundary: value judgment, ethical limits, emotional resonance — what AI lacks, and where human distinctiveness sits.
- Use this when asked about Human-Centered AI, whether AI replaces humans, or why human judgment still matters. Keep it short unless they ask for depth.
